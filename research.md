Incorporating Local Communication into Multi-Agent RL Agents in a Gridworld

Gridworld Multi-Agent Environment

Figure: Example of a large-scale gridworld environment with numerous agents (each colored block) distributed across the landscape. In our setup, multiple reinforcement learning agents inhabit a gridworld – a grid-based environment where each cell can contain resources (like food/energy) or agents. Agents move around the grid to forage and survive ￼. Such environments can be very large and populous; for example, one recent simulation uses a 1,000,000-cell grid with over 60,000 agents competing for resources ￼. Toolkits like DIRT even support dynamic worlds of size 1024×1024 with thousands of agents interacting simultaneously ￼. Initially, agents improve only via natural selection (survival and reproduction), but we propose to use reinforcement learning (RL) so agents update their policies from reward feedback rather than just “live or die” outcomes ￼. This provides a foundation to introduce explicit communication and social behaviors.

Behavior-Based Communication Cues

A key addition is a mechanism for agents to communicate or signal information to each other. Rather than complex language, we use simple, behavior-based cues that an RL policy can learn to interpret. Two straightforward approaches are:
	•	Explicit message signals: Equip agents with an internal message channel they can control. For example, in JaxLife each agent maintains a small “self-message” vector that it can broadcast to nearby peers ￼. Neighbors receive this as an “other-message” in their observations. This enables agents to send encoded information (e.g. a number or bit) to one another as part of their action output ￼. An RL policy can learn to set and read these messages to convey meaning (like a simple code for “I found food” or “I need help”). Because the message is part of the agent’s action-space, using it has an energy or opportunity cost, encouraging purposeful communication.
	•	Environmental cues (smell/sound): Another lightweight method is to let agents produce sensory cues in the environment that others can detect. For instance, the DIRT simulator provides smell and audio channels that agents can emit and sense ￼. A smell can persist in nearby grid cells and drift with wind, while an audio signal propagates quickly but dissipates over distance ￼. An agent’s policy might learn to release a “scent” when it discovers food or to make a sound when low on energy. Nearby agents sensing these cues can then infer information (like the location of food or a neighbor’s distress) without direct verbal messages. These cues are inherently behavior-based – they arise from an agent’s actions in the world and can be evolved or learned as simple communication protocols.

By using one or both of the above mechanisms, we keep communication simple and emergent. Agents don’t use any human language; they either set a numeric signal or perform an action that creates a perceivable cue. The meaning of the signals is learned through RL – e.g. the policy might learn that emitting a certain value or smell in a specific context leads to a beneficial outcome if other agents respond to it.

Local Communication Protocol

It’s important that communication remains local rather than global broadcast. In a gridworld, this means an agent can only reach others in its vicinity with its messages or cues. We implement locality in the communication mechanism as follows:
	•	Neighborhood range: Define a radius (in grid cells) or nearest-neighbor rule for communication. Agents will only send/receive messages with others within that local range. For example, JaxLife agents send their message to only the closest neighboring agents ￼ rather than to all agents in the world. Similarly, a smell or sound in DIRT would only travel so far or would diffuse, so only agents in a local region pick it up ￼. This ensures each agent’s communication influences its immediate community, not the entire population.
	•	Update protocol: Communication can be integrated into the agents’ step-wise interaction. At each time step, after agents choose their actions, the environment updates the communication channels. For explicit messages, if an agent chooses a “send” action, the simulator delivers that agent’s self-message to its nearby peers, updating those peers’ observations (their “other-message” field) ￼. For environmental cues, if an agent emits a signal, the environment places that signal in the grid (e.g. a smell concentration in the agent’s cell), which then spreads to adjacent cells. In the next observation cycle, any agent within range (e.g. within a certain Manhattan distance or in the same cell) will sense the signal’s presence/intensity.
	•	Limited capacity: Keeping messages simple (e.g. a small vector or single value) and signals ephemeral enforces that only coarse information can be shared. This nudges agents to evolve efficient protocols. For instance, with a 1-bit message an agent might use 0/1 to indicate two conditions (like “I’m fine” vs. “Need assistance”), whereas a smell cue might just indicate “food here” if above a threshold. The JaxLife study indeed found that over time agents became more sensitive to their communication channel inputs, meaning they learned to use those bits of information to influence behavior ￼ ￼.

By confining signals locally, we mirror how real social organisms communicate in proximity (through pheromones, sounds, or gestures). This local protocol encourages formation of neighbor interactions and prevents any single agent from globally dominating communication.

Sharing Energy and Position Information

What information should the agents actually convey? A simple and useful choice is to have agents share their own state – specifically their energy level (or hunger) and relative position – with nearby agents. We can implement this in a straightforward way leveraging the communication mechanism and even “oracle” shortcuts:
	•	State as part of observation: The environment can directly include nearby agents’ key attributes in an agent’s observation. For example, at each timestep an agent might observe the attributes of the closest agents around it ￼. These attributes can include the other agents’ current energy (or health) and their position relative to the observing agent. In practice, we can supply a vector like (Δx, Δy, energy) for each neighbor within a small radius. This is akin to an oracle telling the agent “A neighbor is 2 cells east (Δx=2) and 1 north (Δy=-1) with energy 5”. By building this into the perception, we ensure the agent has access to the information that communication would provide.
	•	Explicit messaging of state: Alternatively, if using the explicit message channel, we can design it so that an agent’s self-message encodes its own state. For instance, the agent’s message vector could be [normalized_energy, pos_x, pos_y] or some encoded form of its location. Then a “send” action broadcasts this vector to neighbors, who receive it as the other-message input. This way, agents actively transmit their vital stats. Since the user allows using an oracle instead of actual speech, we don’t require the agents to learn a language for “I have X energy”; we directly map their state into the communication channel in a predefined way. The RL policy’s job is then to interpret the received information and take advantage of it (for example, moving toward a neighbor that signals low energy to share food, or clustering if a neighbor signals its location).
	•	Inference of own position: By exchanging position data, agents can even help each other localize. If an agent lacks an absolute coordinate system, hearing relative positions from several neighbors (or a landmark agent) could allow it to infer its own location. In our simple formulation, however, we assume each agent can measure positions relative to itself (so it knows Δx, Δy to others). The communicated positions mainly help agents know where others are – useful for alignment and grouping.

This sharing of energy and position addresses the core of “inferring from others.” Each agent gains situational awareness: it can gauge if nearby allies are starving or energetic, and it knows who is nearby and roughly where. All of this is achieved through local communication channels (or oracle-assisted observations) that RL policies can learn to utilize. Notably, JaxLife’s agents in a similar setting receive observations of their neighbors’ attributes ￼, which proved sufficient for them to develop coordinated behaviors over generations.

Reward Design for Community Behavior

To encourage agents to actually use communication and cooperate, we introduce a reward signal tied to social interaction. In reinforcement learning, designing an appropriate reward function is crucial. We add a “community happiness” reward that grants agents an extra incentive for being near others or helping others. Specifically:
	•	Clustering reward: An agent receives a small positive reward for having other agents within a certain radius. For example, at each timestep, +0.1 reward for each neighbor within 2 grid cells. This directly rewards agents for flocking together (being “around more agents,” as suggested). Over time, an RL policy will learn that it gains higher cumulative reward by staying with the group rather than wandering alone. This induces a form of artificial herd instinct. We must be careful to balance this so it doesn’t override survival – e.g. an agent shouldn’t starve just to stay with others – but tuning the weight of the reward can achieve a healthy trade-off.
	•	Communication use reward: We can also reward informative communication acts. For instance, if an agent emits a signal that ends up increasing another agent’s survival (say Agent A calls others to a food source, and Agent B actually arrives and eats), Agent A could get a small reward as credit. This is harder to assign (it might require tracing which signals led to positive outcomes), but even a simpler proxy could work: give a tiny reward each time an agent sends a message or cue while near others. This encourages agents to at least attempt communication. The usefulness of the communication will then be indirectly reinforced by the survival/resource rewards that result from better coordination.
	•	Help and reciprocity: Optionally, one can design rewards for altruistic behaviors. For example, if an agent transfers energy to a weaker neighbor (if such action is possible in the environment), reward the giver as well as the receiver. However, in our simple gridworld we assume direct transfers aren’t implemented; instead, helping is implicit (like leading someone to food or jointly attacking a threat). Thus, the main social reward remains proximity (as a proxy for cooperation) and possibly successful group outcomes (like group survives an attack).

By adding these social rewards, we shift the learning objective to value collective outcomes alongside individual survival. Each agent’s policy is now optimizing not just for eating and reproducing, but also for forming beneficial interactions. This is a departure from the pure evolution setup (where only offspring count). In an RL context, similar ideas have been explored in multi-agent environments where agents get shared or shaped rewards to induce teamwork. For instance, in Neural MMO (a multi-agent game environment), agents are trained with explicit reward functions (e.g. for survival and exploration) rather than just natural selection ￼. We follow that paradigm by defining a reward that makes “being social” advantageous.

Putting It All Together

With the above components, we have a blueprint for adding communication to agents in a gridworld:
	1.	Observation Space: Extend each agent’s observation to include local other-agent information (neighbor count, relative positions, neighbor energy levels, or received message vector) ￼. This gives agents the sensory inputs needed to detect others and any signals.
	2.	Action Space: Add an action (or continuous output) for communication. In practice this could be a scalar broadcast signal or a small vector that the agent can output each timestep. If this action is used, the content is either predefined (e.g. agent’s own state) or learned (arbitrary bit pattern). The environment delivers this to neighbors as described ￼. For environmental cues, the action might be “emit smell” or “emit sound” with certain intensity, which the environment handles by diffusion.
	3.	Local Communication Rules: Enforce that communication effects are local. The environment checks which agents are within range and only those agents’ observations get updated with the message or cue. This can be the single nearest agent (as in a pairwise message passing ￼) or all agents in a radius. Ensure that signals decay over distance or time so information is not unlimited.
	4.	Reward Function: Define the reward each agent gets at each step. This includes the primary survival-based rewards (e.g. +1 for eating food, -1 if starving, etc.) and new social rewards. For example, +R for each neighbor nearby (to promote grouping) and perhaps +R_small for sending a communication when neighbors are present (to encourage using the channel). The reward shaping should be dense enough that the agent gets frequent feedback for being social, guiding the RL learning process.
	5.	Learning Algorithm: Train the agents’ policies with a multi-agent RL algorithm (e.g. independent PPO or Q-learning for each agent, or a joint policy if appropriate). The policy will update its weights to maximize the cumulative reward, which now implicitly requires balancing individual goals (energy collection) with social goals (staying with group, communicating useful information). Over time, we expect the population to learn behaviors like clustering together when advantageous, calling out when they find resources or when they are low on energy, etc., because these behaviors yield higher rewards.

Through these steps, the agents move from a purely evolutionary paradigm to a reinforcement learning paradigm with communication. They still must survive (find energy, avoid death), but now they can leverage shared information to do so more effectively. We anticipate seeing phenomena such as agents signaling before they die (a cue of low energy), agents gravitating towards others to form stable groups (since being near others is rewarded and can aid survival), and possibly rudimentary coordination (one agent leads others to food or collectively they chase a prey). The simplicity of our communication (no complex language, just local signals) keeps the learning problem tractable, yet it provides a foundation for emergent social behavior.

Ultimately, this approach allows agents to infer important state from their peers and adapt their policies accordingly. Communication becomes an acquired skill: the RL policies update so that agents who listen and respond to others do better, and agents who send helpful signals are rewarded. Over many training episodes, this should produce a self-organizing community of agents that use basic communication for improved collective outcomes, validating the idea of an “artificial community happiness” driving more intelligent group dynamics.

These systems demonstrate that even simple message passing or cues in a gridworld can lead to non-trivial emergent behaviors. By adding explicit rewards and using RL for policy updates (similar to approaches in other multi-agent RL environments ￼), we ensure that agents learn to actively use and respond to communication, rather than relying solely on natural selection to incidentally favor communicative traits. The result is a straightforward yet powerful extension to the gridworld agents: they not only live and die by their actions, but also speak and listen in a minimalist way, improving their survival as a community.
